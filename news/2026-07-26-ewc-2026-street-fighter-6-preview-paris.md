Xiaohai is the two-time defending Street Fighter 6 champion at the Esports World Cup, and he almost did not get to defend anything. When he left KuaiShou Gaming for Team Falcons, he lost the automatic re-entry that comes with the title, and had to qualify for the Esports World Cup 2026 Street Fighter 6 field the same way as everyone else chasing him. He made it, but only after a stretch that included a ninth-place finish at DreamHack Atlanta and a 193rd-place exit at Evo Japan. That is the story heading into Paris.

## A Trophy That Keeps Finding the Same Hands

The Esports World Cup only started running Street Fighter 6 in 2024, but its predecessor event, Gamers8, crowned Kakeru back in 2023. Since the Esports World Cup name took over, Xiaohai has won every single edition, first over Kawano 5-2 in 2024, then in a five-game comeback from a 3-0 deficit against Blaz in 2025 that made him the first back-to-back champion in the tournament's history.

The prize pool sits at $1,000,000 for 2026, with the champion collecting $250,000, the runner-up $130,000, third place $70,000, and fourth $50,000. That champion's share is down from the $300,000 Xiaohai banked in 2024, a reflection of a smaller bracket rather than a smaller pool. Organizers cut the field from 48 players to 32 this year, tightening qualification and, with it, the number of easy early rounds available to shake off nerves.

The format keeps its three-stage identity. Thirty-two players split into four groups of eight, each playing a double-elimination, best-of-five GSL-style bracket, with the top four from each group advancing. Those sixteen survivors split into two groups of eight for a second double-elimination stage, again best-of-five, sending the top four from each into the final eight. From there, it is single elimination, best-of-nine, all the way to the championship, with no bracket reset. A double-elimination structure would let players survive one bad set. This format punishes it outright, favoring stamina and composure over the safety net a loser's bracket usually provides.

## The Favorites: A Champion and the Man Who Already Beat Him

Xiaohai's case rests on precedent nobody else in the field can claim. He has solved this exact format twice, and both times his form peaked precisely when it mattered, not before. His 2026 season backs that pattern up in an odd way. After the DreamHack Atlanta and Evo Japan stumbles, he turned it around at Combo Breaker, beating Hinao 3-1 in the grand final before resetting the bracket with a 3-0 sweep, his first major Street Fighter 6 title of the year and the result that punched his ticket to Paris.

Blaz is the man standing in the way of easy math. He took Xiaohai to the brink in last year's grand final, and he has since done more than that. At Capcom Cup 12, Blaz beat Xiaohai 3-2 in the winners' finals before ultimately finishing runner-up to Sahara. That head-to-head result this year matters more than the 2025 final does. Blaz has already shown he can beat the champion when it counts, not just push him.

## The Disruptors: A Fresh Trophy and a Historic Run

Sahara arrives as the reigning Capcom Cup 12 champion, a result he earned by going through Blaz in March, though it is no longer the newest form story in the field. MenaRD has since topped it twice over. He won Evo 2026 outright in June, beating a field of 2,416 entrants, and became the first player to win Evo and Evo Japan back to back. He qualified for Paris through his team's run at the Street Fighter League World Championship 2025, not through that Evo win, but the form is the form, and it is more current than Sahara's.

## The Pick: Betting on Proof of Concept

The hardest part of picking this bracket is that nobody else has done what Xiaohai has done under this specific format. A no-reset, best-of-nine top eight rewards players who have already proven they can win long sets when the alternative is going home, and Xiaohai has done exactly that in both prior finals.

It is not a clean case. Blaz has already beaten him once in 2026, and Sahara walks in with real hardware of his own. But betting against a two-time champion who turned a rough year around at exactly the right moment, in exactly the format he has already mastered twice, is a harder argument to make than betting on him for the three-peat.
